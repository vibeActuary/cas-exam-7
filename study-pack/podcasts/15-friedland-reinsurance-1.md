# Episode 15 — Friedland, reinsurance types and data

**Hosts:** Riley and Sam  
**Length:** about 18 minutes  
**Tasks:** A15, A17  
**Paper:** Friedland 2022 study note

---

**Riley:** A17 first. What is reinsurance for, in one breath?

**Sam:** Insurance for insurers. Spread risk, protect surplus, stabilize results, increase capacity, support withdrawal from a line, provide expertise or capital relief. If you only say “they cede bad losses,” you missed capacity and surplus protection.

**Riley:** Types I must define.

**Sam:** Quota share: a fixed percent of every loss, and usually of premium. Cleanest reserving. Ceded is about that percent of gross if the terms align. Watch ceding commission, sliding scale, loss corridors, and caps.

Surplus share: the percent varies by policy limit versus a line. The ceded percentage is not one number.

Per-risk or per-occurrence excess: pays above a retention on one risk or one occurrence. Thin data, long development, clash, fight over the definition of occurrence.

Catastrophe excess: pays above a retention on an event. Few points, demand surge, reinstatements, hours clause.

Aggregate or stop-loss: pays when the subject loss ratio or subject dollars exceed a threshold. Binary. IBNR in the layer depends on how close the subject is to the attachment.

Facultative: one risk. Almost no triangle. Exposure, BF, ground-up implied.

Treaty: a book. You may have a triangle. It is still not a primary triangle.

Retrocession: reinsurance of reinsurers. Thinner, more clash, more contract complexity.

Also: prospective versus retrospective — loss portfolio transfer, adverse development cover. Losses-occurring versus risks-attaching. Multi-year. Finite or structured risk transfer — you still need a reserve; the accounting is not a free lunch.

**Riley:** Losses-occurring versus risks-attaching, quickly.

**Sam:** Losses-occurring: the treaty covers losses that occur during the treaty term, regardless of when the underlying policy was written. Risks-attaching: it covers policies that incept during the treaty term, and those policies’ losses can run long after the treaty year. Your “accident year” is not their “treaty year.” If you mix them in one triangle you have an A1 disaster.

**Riley:** A15. Why can’t I chain-ladder the ceded triangle?

**Sam:** Primary methods assume a stable, high-volume, homogeneous triangle. Reinsurance usually has low frequency and high severity — one claim owns a year. Reporting lag is longer: the primary has to know, report to the reinsurer, the reinsurer opens a file. Bordereaux are late or at the wrong limit. Program structure changes: last year’s five-xs-five is this year’s ten-xs-ten. You cannot stack those years without restating. Clash and cat create dependence across years and across cedents. Inuring reinsurance means the subject is already net of something else. Commutations, sunsets, aggregates, reinstatements shape the ultimate. And premium develops — adjustable premium, reinstatement premium, sliding-scale commission. That is Teng and Perkins on a treaty.

**Riley:** Adjustments before I steal a primary method.

**Sam:** Restate history onto the current structure — same attachment, limit, basis. Separate attritional working layer from cat and clash. Use exposure: on-level subject premium, limits profiles, ILFs. That is Clark Cape Cod and BF territory, not chain ladder. Develop reported counts and average severity separately when dollars are unusable. Apply a report-lag factor when the triangle is empty. Gross-up from a trusted primary ultimate with a size model — Sahasrabuddhe — if you are the cedent. Credibility-weight any thin treaty triangle toward the exposure method — Brosius and Benktander instinct.

**Riley:** Exam grab bag.

**Sam:** Define quota share, surplus, per-risk excess, cat excess, stop-loss, facultative versus treaty, losses-occurring versus risks-attaching. Give four reasons a primary chain ladder on a ceded triangle is biased. Name three adjustments: restate structure, split cat, use exposure.

**Riley:** Next episode we actually book the ceded number.

**Sam:** Cedent versus reinsurer. Different data, different method. Do not mix them up.
