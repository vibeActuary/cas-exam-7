# Episode 05 — Development by layer

**Hosts:** Riley and Sam  
**Length:** about 17 minutes  
**Tasks:** A4  
**Paper:** Sahasrabuddhe 2010, revised 2013

---

**Riley:** Cook nineteen seventy said do not double-count trend and development. The overlap fallacy. Sahasrabuddhe says they still talk to each other.

**Sam:** They do not overlap. They are connected. The connector is the claim-size model. Limited development factors move when inflation walks the size distribution through a deductible or a limit. If you ignore that, your excess LDFs and your ILFs are internally inconsistent. That is the whole paper.

**Riley:** Layer language.

**Sam:** Layer L is d to p. Truncated below at attachment d, censored above at limit p. Ground-up unlimited is d equals zero, p equals infinity. S of layer A versus layer B is the ratio of limited expected values. LEV to p-A minus LEV to d-A, over LEV to p-B minus LEV to d-B.

**Riley:** The conversion I will be asked to compute.

**Sam:** You have a basic-limit development pattern F-B, at a reference cost level. You want the development factor for layer X. F-X equals F-B times S at ultimate of X versus B, divided by S at the current age of X versus B.

In words: take the basic factor, multiply by how big the target layer is relative to basic at ultimate, divide by how big it is relative to basic at this age.

**Riley:** Why is the excess twelve-to-ultimate bigger?

**Sam:** Because a lot of what is going to pierce the attachment has not pierced it yet. Inflation and late reporting move mass into the layer later. A ground-up basic-limit factor applied to a one-million xs one-million layer is usually too small. You understate excess unpaid. That is the A4 trap.

**Riley:** Shortcut if I only have an ultimate size model and one diagonal of observed layer ratios.

**Sam:** Replace the age-specific S in the denominator with R-sub-j, the observed ratio of layer X to basic on the current diagonal. Justified when you are willing to say cost-level differences in the *ratio* of layers are immaterial. Say that sentence if you use it.

**Riley:** Procedure on a spreadsheet item.

**Sam:** One: name the base layer B — where the triangle is credible — and the target layer X. Two: trend the size-model parameters with ground-up unlimited cost-level indices. Lognormal mu shifts by ln T. Pareto or exponential scale moves with T. Three: adjust the historical triangle to a common limit and cost level with LEV ratios before you pick LDFs. Four: convert the basic pattern with the S ratio. Five: apply those layer-specific LDFs to the target layer’s current diagonal.

**Riley:** Consistency checks.

**Sam:** Unlimited ground-up, and trend only in the accident-year direction: ordinary LDFs are fine. That is the special case, not the general one. Higher attachment, larger tail LDF. Higher trend, larger excess LDF. ILFs, trend, and LDFs must come from the same size model. If pricing used Pareto alpha 1.5 and reserving used lognormal, you have an A4 inconsistency even if each piece looks reasonable.

**Riley:** Pinto and Gogol?

**Sam:** Different paper. They fit excess LDFs versus retention from a giant industry database. Sahasrabuddhe is for the actuary who has a size model and a primary pattern and does not have that database. Do not describe the wrong method.

**Riley:** Exam grab bag.

**Sam:** Why does a high excess layer have a larger twelve-to-ult than a basic limit? Size model plus trend. Write F-X equals F-B times S-infinity over S-at-age. Name the three objects that must be consistent: development, trend, size model. Cook killed the overlap fallacy; Sahasrabuddhe added the connector.

**Riley:** Repeat the conversion.

**Sam:** Target LDF equals basic LDF times S at ultimate over S at this age. Excess develops longer.
