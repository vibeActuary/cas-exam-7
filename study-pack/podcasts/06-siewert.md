# Episode 06 — Siewert, WC high deductibles

**Hosts:** Riley and Sam  
**Length:** about 16 minutes  
**Tasks:** A4  
**Paper:** Siewert 1996

---

**Riley:** Is this just Sahasrabuddhe with a hard hat?

**Sam:** Same connector, dirtier data. A high-deductible workers-comp policy is not “primary minus a number.” The deductible is per-occurrence, sometimes with an aggregate. The excess is often unlimited medical. Development is extremely long. And the mix of small medical-only claims versus large lost-time and occupational-disease claims changes what lives in the deductible versus the excess as the year ages.

**Riley:** Split.

**Sam:** Limited layer: what the insured retains. Expected cost is a limited expected value, E of X wedge D. Excess layer: what the insurer or excess writer pays. Expected cost is E of X minus D, positive part.

Development happens for three reasons. Counts still report. Known claims still develop in severity. And a claim that looked small can pierce the deductible later. That last one is Sahasrabuddhe’s connector wearing a WC badge. Therefore you cannot take a ground-up LDF and apply it to both layers. The excess LDF is larger and longer.

**Riley:** Methods he actually discusses.

**Sam:** Direct excess triangle — if you have excess-only data. Often thin. High process variance. Credibility-weight toward a severity-model excess pattern. That is Brosius’s instinct. Do not take a five-year weighted excess LDF of 4.00 and call it science.

Ground-up minus deductible layer — build a ground-up ultimate, subtract a modeled deductible-layer ultimate. Consistent only if both pieces use the same size model.

Increased-limit or excess-ratio method — an excess ratio that itself develops with age.

Report-lag plus severity-lag — especially for occupational disease and lifetime medical.

**Riley:** WC landmines.

**Sam:** Medical-only versus lost-time mix. A shift toward medical-only cheapens the deductible layer and can starve or feed the excess depending on whether the large medical claims are the excess.

Deductible erosion. A fixed five-hundred-thousand deductible on a ten-year-old year is a different layer than five-hundred-thousand today. Trend the attachment.

Aggregate deductibles and corridors. The per-occurrence model is not enough. You need frequency.

ALAE inside or outside the deductible. If you apply a loss LDF to a loss-and-ALAE layer without saying so, you will lose the basis points.

**Riley:** Reasonableness.

**Sam:** Excess unpaid over ground-up unpaid should rise as you look at more recent, less mature years — more of the remaining development is excess. If the insured’s retention triangle is developing up while your excess is developing down, check recoveries and reimbursements before you pick a factor. Compare implied excess ratios to the pricing ILF table. Persistent disagreement is a model problem, not a “midpoint” problem.

**Riley:** Exam grab bag.

**Sam:** Why ground-up LDFs understate excess unpaid on high-deductible WC. Sketch limited LEV versus excess tail, separate development. Name two WC reasons the layer mix changes with age: lost-time emergence, medical inflation, reopenings, occupational disease. Thin excess triangle: credibility to a severity pattern, not a wild LDF.

**Riley:** One sentence I can write in my sleep.

**Sam:** Excess WC deductibles develop longer than ground-up because late severity and inflation push claims through the retention, so I will not apply the ground-up LDF to the excess layer.
